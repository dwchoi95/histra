Paper keys used throughout (all full texts were read directly from arXiv PDFs; section and page numbers refer to the arXiv versions):

## Q1. Who rated, how many raters, how recruited, compensation?

### Takeaway
The Phung/Singla papers use very small expert panels. [P23] and [P24] each use 2 experts who are also the "human tutor" baseline. [PyFiXV] has 2 experts calibrate on a subset, and then a single expert rates everything. In [R24], two authors (a TA and a lecturer) did the expert coding, and 3 students rated hints in situ. [B25] is the only study with a large, external, recruited panel: 41 Java educators completed the task, recruited via mailing lists, forums and social media, with no reward. None of the papers reports paying expert raters.

### Cited Findings
**[P23] Phung et al. 2023 (benchmark)**
- Raters: "We have n_evals = 2 human evaluators who provide annotations… this set of n_evals = 2 human evaluators is same as n_Tutor = 2 human experts that are part of the Tutor method" (§2, "Metrics and evaluation process", p.4). — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- The Tutor experts are described as "human experts with experience in Python programming and tutoring introductory programming classes" who may use a debugger, the web or course materials (§2, "Methods evaluated", p.4). — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- Self-rating is avoided. Each evaluator annotates ChatGPT, GPT-4 and Tutor outputs, but for Tutor "only for the n_Tutor − 1 human experts by excluding the evaluator themselves" (§2, p.4). — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- The paper lists as a limitation: "our work involved only two human experts acting as tutors and evaluators… our evaluation only considered expert-based assessments and didn't involve students" (§9, p.14). — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- Recruitment and compensation: not reported. Whether the evaluators are co-authors: not reported.

**[P24] Phung et al. LAK 2024**
- "two human evaluators independently rated the feedback generated by techniques" (§4.3, p.10). Footnote 7: "these two human evaluators are same as two human tutors employed in the TutorHints technique. When evaluating TutorHints technique, an evaluator does not assess their own feedback produced while acting as a tutor" (p.11). — [P24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03780)
- Tutors are "human tutors with experience in Python programming and tutoring" (§4.2). — [P24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03780)
- Recruitment and compensation: not reported.

**[PyFiXV] Phung et al. EDM 2023**
- Reference-feedback annotation: "Three experts, with extensive experience in Python programming and tutoring, provided annotations" of fixed program plus explanation for all 480 programs, which are used as few-shot examples (§4.2). — [PyFiXV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04662)
- Quality rating: "two experts rated 100 generated feedback instances… Afterward, one expert (with experience in tutoring Python programming classes) did these feedback ratings for the evaluation results" (§4.2). — [PyFiXV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04662)
- Recruitment and compensation: not reported.

**[HiB] Kotalwar et al. NeurIPS 2024**
- Reported (via fetched summary of §5.2): two evaluators, "one author and one external researcher", independently rated 90 generated feedback instances for a reliability check, and each final instance then received one annotation. — [HiB](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05053v2)

**[R24] Roest et al. ACE'24**
- Expert raters: "two experts: two authors of this paper with extensive experience as a TA and a lecturer, respectively" (§3.3.2). — [R24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10055)
- Student raters: "Based on convenience sampling, we recruited three first-year Bachelor of Artificial Intelligence students at Utrecht University," who "already had some programming experience and were taking a Python course" (§3.3.1). — [R24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10055)
- Compensation: not reported.

**[B25] Brown et al. (Howzat?)**
- Recruitment: "We recruited participants to act as judges via mailing lists, forums and social media. We asked for 'Java educators' to complete an online task plus survey. There was no reward for participation" (§4.4, p.10). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- Funnel: "85 participants signed up; three started but did not finish the task, 41 completed the task, and 35 of those completed the survey" (§4.4). The abstract says "We recruited 44 Java educators." — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- Participants were assigned round-robin to Snapshots. Four Snapshots reached the required 8 completions (8, 10, 8, 9 judges). Two Snapshots had only 3 completions each and were excluded from the ranking analysis (§4.4, p.12). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- Expertise was measured by free-text descriptions that the researchers then ranked by comparative judgement. Median: "Teaching Java for more than twenty years…". Lower quartile: "taught Java programming to High School students about 8 years" (§5). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- The human hint authors were five researchers "who are also experienced programming educators with significant teaching experience". They wrote hints independently: "no researchers saw another researcher's hints until they had completed writing their own" (§3.2–3.3). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)

### Inferences
- The abstract's "44" probably counts the 41 completers plus the 3 who started and did not finish. 35 judges went into the ranking analysis (8+10+8+9), and 6 more came from the two excluded Snapshots. This is my reconciliation; the paper does not state it.
- In the Phung/Singla line, the accepted standard for an expert evaluation is 2 experienced Python tutors. They are often also the human baseline, with the "no self-rating" rule. Reviewers at LAK, EDM and ICER accepted this, while the authors flagged it as a limitation.
- For a small repair evaluation (for example, TAs rating histra patches), the [P23]/[P24] design is the closest precedent: 2 experts, an independent pass over all items, and averaging. The [PyFiXV]/[R24]/[HiB] design is cheaper: 2 experts double-rate a calibration subset, report kappa, then one expert rates the rest.

### Gaps
- None of the Phung/Singla papers report compensation, how the experts were recruited, or whether the experts are co-authors. [P23] does not even state the tutors' institutions.

## Q2. How many items were rated, and how were they sampled?

### Takeaway
The item counts are small: 25 instances in [P23], 79 buggy programs in [P24], 60 test programs per fold in [PyFiXV], 48 hints in [R24], and 100 hints (4 snapshots × 25) in [B25]. Sampling is mostly purposive ("capture different types of bugs") or "median submission per student". [B25] is the only one that sampled sessions at random from a large corpus (Blackbox) before selecting stuck states by hand.

### Cited Findings
**[P23]**
- 5 problems from geeksforgeeks.org (GCD, FIBONACCI, DIVISORSDIV3, PALINDROME, MERGESTRS) and 5 buggy programs each, giving 25. Programs were "picked… to capture different types of bugs for each problem and ensure that these programs are associated with submissions by different users". They range "from 4 to 31 lines of code" (§2, p.2–3, Fig. 2). — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- Per instance, ChatGPT and GPT-4 each produce 1 output (n=1 query, through the web UI), and Tutor produces 2 outputs (2 experts independently). "For a given method and scenario, we have 25 total instances for evaluation" (§2, p.4). Footnote 5 cites the GPT-4 cap of "25 messages every 3 hours". — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- The pair-programming scenario derives partial programs from the 25 buggy programs "by removing the second half of the program" (§6, footnote 8). — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)

**[P24]**
- Three datasets, 79 buggy programs in total (§5: "small datasets comprising a total of 79 buggy programs"):
  - BasicAlgo: the same 25 programs as [P23].
  - DataRegex: 24 buggy submissions, each from a unique student, from an online data-science master's course.
  - DataAnalysis: 30 buggy programs.
  - Sampling rule: "if there are multiple buggy submissions, we take only the median submission w.r.t to submission times" (§4.1, p.9–10). — [P24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03780)
- Techniques rated: GPT4Hints-Base, -IO, -IOFix, TutorHints, GPT4Hints-GPT3.5Val, plus 6 validation variants (Figs. 6–7). The total number of rated hints is not reported. — [P24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03780)

**[PyFiXV]**
- Two datasets of 240 programs each with syntax errors and token length ≤ 500:
  - TigerJython: Python 2, private and anonymized.
  - Codeforces: Python 3, restricted to contests from July 2021 onward to avoid Codex training-data leakage.
- Each dataset is split 25% D_test, 50% D_shot and 25% D_cal, with 4-fold cross-validation and non-overlapping D_test. "evaluating metrics on TigerJython dataset for PyFiXV requires 480 feedback ratings (4×60 for D_test and 4×60 for D_cal)" (§4.2). — [PyFiXV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04662)

**[HiB]**
- Evaluation sets: 25 buggy programs (BasicAlgo), 25 (IntroPyNUS) and 21 (KarelAlgo). Hint results come from the first run only "because of the annotation effort", while repair metrics are averaged over 3 runs (§5.1–5.3, per fetched summary). — [HiB](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05053v2)

**[R24]**
- One exercise (CodingBat "clumpCount"). The 3 students requested 11, 20 and 17 hints, 48 in total (§5.2). "All 48 generated hints were classified by one or two experts" (§5.3). — [R24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10055)
- Calibration subset: "we randomly selected 19 hints to compare" (§3.3.2). — [R24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10055)

**[B25]**
- Snapshots: "we randomly sampled sessions from the Blackbox dataset… (21st November 2023), manually selecting states where we inferred that the students were stuck… as evidenced by making no productive progress for some time after that point" (§3.1). Eight snapshots were prepared and four were analysed (§8). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- 25 hints per snapshot: 4 LLMs (Mixtral-8x7B, Gemini, GPT-3.5, GPT-4) × 5 prompts, plus 5 human hints. That gives "100 hints ranked by more than 32 educators" (Fig. 1, §4.3). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- Light post-processing: unified code formatting, and boilerplate greetings and sign-offs removed. Hints that addressed the educator ("prompt misfiring") were kept on purpose to penalise them (§4.3). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- Internal inconsistency: §4.10 writes "(5 AI models × 4 prompts + 5 human hints = 25 Generators)", whereas §4.3 and Fig. 1 say 4 models × 5 prompts. — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)

### Inferences
- In [P23], each evaluator rated about 25 × 3 = 75 outputs per scenario (ChatGPT, GPT-4, and the other tutor). I derived this from §2; the paper does not state it.
- A histra-style study with about 25–80 trajectories × 2–3 systems is in the same size range as [P23]/[P24] and would be considered normal in this literature.

### Gaps
- [P24] does not report the total number of rated hints across all techniques and variants.
- [R24] does not say which 29 hints went to which single author, beyond "one author rated the remaining 29".

## Q3. Rating dimensions, scales and rubric wording (correctness, closeness to student code, minimality, usefulness)

### Takeaway
The Phung/Singla line uses binary attributes combined by AND into an "Overall" or "Good" flag. Program repairs themselves are not rated by humans in [P23]. They are scored automatically with **Correct** (test suite) and **EditTokens** (token-level Levenshtein distance to the buggy program). [PyFiXV] turns "small number of edits" into an expert-rated binary attribute. [P23]'s pair-programming scenario has a manually annotated **ContextKept** attribute ("keeps the context of the partial program, e.g., variable names"), the closest human-rated analogue of intent preservation. [R24] uses 9 categorical/binary criteria (including "Personalised": refers to the student's code or approach) and 5-point Likert ratings from students. [B25] uses no rubric, only a holistic "which is the best hint" instruction.

### Cited Findings
**[P23] Program repair scenario (§3, p.5)**
- Repair prompt: "Can you help in fixing this program with as few changes as possible?… Make sure that you make minimal possible changes needed to fix the program" (Fig. 4). The same text serves as the instructions to the human tutors (§2). — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- "**Correct** (binary, 1 value being better) captures whether the generated program is correct w.r.t. the problem specification; we use automated test suites." — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- "**EditTokens** (non-negative number, lower value being better) captures the token-based edit distance between the generated program and the buggy program." Footnote 6: "measured by first tokenizing programs using Pygments library and then computing Levenshtein edit-distance over token strings." — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- "in this particular scenario, human evaluators computed these attributes using automated scripts without requiring manual annotation." — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)

**[P23] Pair programming scenario (§6, p.10–11)**
- Correct (automated).
- "**ContextKept** (binary, 1 value being better) captures whether the completed program keeps the context of the partial program, e.g., variable names." This is the manually annotated attribute.
- EditLines is the line-based edit distance (automated).
- "Overall… is 1 only when both the Correct and ContextKept attributes are 1."
- Source: [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)

**[P23] Hint generation scenario (§4, p.6)**
- "All attributes for this scenario are binary." The attribute definitions:
  - HCorrect: "provides correct information for resolving issues in the student's buggy program."
  - HInformative: "provides useful information to help the student resolve bug(s); this attribute is set to 0 by default when the hint is incorrect."
  - HConceal: "the information in the generated hint is not too detailed, so the student would also have to reason about implementing the fixes; … set to 0 by default when the hint is incorrect."
  - HComprehensible: "easy to understand, presented in a readable format, and doesn't contain redundant information."
  - HOverall: 1 only if all four hold.
  - ECorrect: the explanation contains correct reasoning.
  - Overall = HOverall ∧ ECorrect.
- "these attributes require manual annotation." — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- Other scenarios use analogous binary rubrics. Contextualized explanation has Correct, Complete and Comprehensible (§7). Grading feedback is compared to the evaluator's own grading via Match and absolute point differences (§5). — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)

**[P24]**
- Reuses the [P23] hint rubric verbatim: HCorrect, HInformative, HConceal and HComprehensible, binary, with HOverall = AND (§2). ECorrect is rated additionally (footnote 8). — [P24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03780)
- Aggregate metrics: "Coverage measuring the percentage number of times the generated feedback is provided to the student; Precision measuring the percentage number of times the provided feedback is of good quality w.r.t. the HOverall quality" (§2). — [P24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03780)
- The repair used inside the pipeline is chosen automatically. It is the program among 10 GPT-4 samples (temperature 0.5) that passes the tests and has "the smallest token-edit distance w.r.t. P_b" (Pygments plus Levenshtein, §3.1). The fix itself is not rated by humans. — [P24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03780)

**[PyFiXV]**
- Feedback = a fixed program plus an explanation. Binary attributes:
  1. "P_f is syntactically correct and is obtained by making a small number of edits to fix P_b"
  2. "X is complete, i.e., contains information about all errors and required fixes"
  3. "X is correct"
  4. "X is comprehensible"
- Good quality = all four hold (§2.1). Footnote 2 says the four attributes are independent, for example "complete" can be true even when the information is wrong. — [PyFiXV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04662)
- The reported average edit distance between the buggy and fixed program was "about 10.4 and 7.5 tokens" (TigerJython, Codeforces) (§4.3). — [PyFiXV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04662)

**[HiB]**
- Hint attributes are HCorrect, HInformative, HConceal and HComprehensible, with HGood = all four. Repairs are scored automatically as RPass (any of n_r candidates passes the tests) and REdit (token-based edit distance from the buggy program) (§3.2, per fetched summary). — [HiB](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05053v2)

**[R24] Expert criteria (Table 2)**
- Feedback type: Keuning et al.'s classification.
- Information: "Does the hint contain additional information, such as an explanation, tip or compliment?"
- Level-of-detail: "Is the hint a bottom-out hint or a high-level description?"
- Personalised: "Does the hint refer to the student's code or approach?"
- Appropriate: "Is the hint a suitable next step, given the current state of the student program?"
- Specific: "Is the hint limited to only one next step?"
- Misleading information: "Does the hint contain misleading information?"
- Tone: direct, neutral or friendly.
- Length: number of sentences.
- Personalised, Appropriate, Specific and Misleading are binary (Fig. 7). — [R24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10055)

**[R24] Student ratings**
- In-tool pop-up after each hint request, with three statements: "The hint is clear", "The hint fits my work", and "The hint is helpful".
- Scale: "short questions with a 5-point Likert scale to limit the resistance to rate feedback", with an optional comment (§3.3.1).
- A post-session interview follows. — [R24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10055)

**[B25]**
- No rubric by design: "We chose not to provide a detailed rubric for judging what makes the best hint."
- Instruction: "Imagine that you are helping a student who is somewhere within their first year of programming instruction, around the ages of 16-18… We want you to determine which is the best hint to give in each circumstance" (§3.4, p.9). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- Judges also received "brief notes with our interpretation of what the problem with the student code was". The notes were not given to the LLMs (§3.4). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- Hint attributes were coded separately by the researchers, not by the judges (§4.7, Table 2):
  - word count;
  - Flesch-Kincaid grade level;
  - VADER sentiment;
  - model;
  - four binary feedback-literacy tags: Telling, Guiding, Developing understanding, Opening up a new perspective;
  - a binary "Partially incorrect" flag ("if any part was incorrect this flag was true").
  - Source: [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)

### Inferences
- For a repair paper, the precedents give three ways to measure "closeness to the student's code":
  - automated token-level Levenshtein distance via Pygments ([P23] EditTokens, [HiB] REdit, [P24]/[PyFiXV] fix selection);
  - an expert-judged binary "small number of edits" attribute ([PyFiXV]);
  - an expert-judged binary "keeps the context (e.g., variable names)" attribute ([P23] ContextKept).
- A histra expert study could reuse ContextKept-style wording, for example "the patch preserves the student's approach/structure", alongside automated EditTokens. That would align directly with the Phung line.
- The "set to 0 by default when incorrect" convention ([P23], [P24]) is a useful rubric rule: it stops an incorrect patch from scoring on secondary attributes.

### Gaps
- No paper in this set has human experts rate the *program repair* itself on minimality, readability or intent. [P23] explicitly automated it, and [PyFiXV] only folds "small number of edits" into one binary attribute together with syntax correctness.
- [P23] does not report the full rater instructions or annotation guide beyond the attribute definitions.

## Q4. Pairwise vs absolute; blinding of the source; randomization of order

### Takeaway
All Phung/Singla papers use absolute binary ratings per output. Only [PyFiXV] explicitly states blinding and randomized cross-condition order. [P23] and [P24] do not report blinding, and their evaluators are themselves the tutors whose outputs are a compared condition. [B25] is the methodological counterpoint: blinded pairwise **adaptive comparative judgement** (No More Marking platform) with arbitrary hint IDs. [R24] has no comparison condition.

### Cited Findings
- **[P23]** uses absolute per-output binary attributes, averaged per method (§2). Blinding and randomization are not reported. — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- **[P24]** uses absolute binary ratings (§2, §4.3). Blinding and randomization are not reported. The evaluators know at least their own tutor outputs, which they skip (footnote 7). — [P24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03780)
- **[PyFiXV]**, footnote 8: "the experts were blinded to the condition (technique) associated with each feedback instance when providing ratings. Moreover, these generated feedback instances were given to experts in randomized order across conditions instead of grouping them per condition." — [PyFiXV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04662)
- **[HiB]**: blinding and randomization were not reported in the fetched content. — [HiB](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05053v2)
- **[R24]**: only one system (StAP-tutor, gpt-3.5-turbo), so blinding does not apply. Students rated each hint right after receiving it, in their own session. — [R24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10055)
- **[B25]** rationale for pairwise over absolute: "It can be difficult to evaluate how good a hint is on such an absolute scale. Is a particular hint a 5/10 or a 6/10?… Instead of 'rate this hint 1–10', the problem becomes 'which of these two hints is better'" (§3.4). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- **[B25]** procedure: "Each educator (judge) is first shown the Snapshot, and then asked to repeatedly rate which of two presented hints are better… with the pairs generated by an adaptive comparative judgement algorithm." The No More Marking platform uses Progressive Adaptive Comparative Judgement and recommends 10 comparisons per item. Hence "≈250 comparisons, which we split across 8 judges doing 31 comparisons each" (§3.4, p.8–9). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- **[B25]** sample-size rationale: citing Verhavert et al., "the number of judges did not impact reliability"; "for 90% reliability, 26 to 37 presentations per item are needed… ranking of N items requires 13N judgements" (§3.4). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- **[B25]** blinding: "All hints were formatted similarly and given arbitrary identifiers to obscure how they might have been generated" (§4.3). The abstract says "Participants ranked the hints without being told how they were generated." Each judge saw only one Snapshot "to reduce the load on each participant" (§3.4, §3.6). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- **[B25]** position bias check: the left-choice rate was compared to a binomial(0.5), "χ² = 44.0, p = 0.06… there was no bias" (§4.5). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)

### Inferences
- For a small expert study comparing histra patches against baselines (for example, an LLM repair or another student's AC), the best-supported design is [PyFiXV]'s: blinded, randomized order mixed across conditions, absolute binary attributes. [B25]'s pairwise comparative judgement suits a "which patch is closer to the student's intent" question, but it needs about 10 comparisons per item. With 3 systems × N programs, pairwise within each program (3 pairs per program) would be a lighter variant. That variant is my suggestion and was not used in these papers.
- Having the evaluators also be the human-tutor baseline ([P23], [P24]) leaves an unblinded-condition risk that reviewers could flag. [PyFiXV]'s footnote 8 is the explicit template to cite.

### Gaps
- None of the Phung/Singla papers report the presentation interface or how outputs were shown (side by side or one at a time).

## Q5. Inter-rater agreement: measure, values, disagreement resolution

### Takeaway
Cohen's kappa is the norm in the Phung/Singla line: 0.72 [PyFiXV], 0.65 [P24], 0.644 [HiB], all described as "substantial". [P23] reports no agreement statistic, only the standard error across 2 evaluators. [R24] reports per-criterion kappa on 19 double-coded hints (0.387–0.883) and resolved disagreements by discussion. [B25] uses comparative-judgement Reliability (0.73, 0.76, 0.76, 0.60 per snapshot) plus judge Infit, and reports 65% raw agreement for the researchers' own hint tagging, with disputes resolved in a meeting.

### Cited Findings
- **[P23]**: no kappa or alpha reported. Results are "aggregate[d] across n_evals human evaluators and report[ed] as mean (stderr)" (§2, p.4).
  - Program repair (Fig. 5a): Correct = ChatGPT 68.0 (0.0), GPT-4 88.0 (0.0), Tutor 100.0 (0.0). EditTokens = 43.0, 36.6, 19.0 (1.2).
  - Hint generation (Fig. 7a), Overall: ChatGPT 18.0 (6.0), GPT-4 66.0 (10.0), Tutor 92.0 (4.0).
  - Source: [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- **[P24]**: "We obtained Cohen's kappa reliability value 0.65 indicating substantial agreement between evaluators" (§4.3, p.11). Which attribute (presumably HOverall) and over which items is not specified. Disagreements are not resolved; results are averaged across the two evaluators as mean (stderr). — [P24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03780)
- **[PyFiXV]**: "we did a smaller scale investigation to establish the rating criteria, where two experts rated 100 generated feedback instances; we obtained Cohen's kappa reliability value 0.72 indicating substantial agreement between experts. Afterward, one expert… did these feedback ratings" (§4.2). — [PyFiXV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04662)
- **[HiB]**: two evaluators on 90 instances, Cohen's kappa 0.644 ("substantial agreement"), then single annotation (§5.2, per fetched summary). — [HiB](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05053v2)
- **[R24]** Table 3, per-criterion agreement on 19 random hints, with Cohen's kappa:

  | Criterion | Agreement | Cohen's kappa |
  |---|---|---|
  | Feedback type | 19/19 | 0.872 |
  | Information | 18/19 | 0.787 |
  | Level-of-detail | 16/19 | 0.387 |
  | Personalised | 18/19 | 0.883 |
  | Appropriate | 18/19 | 0.855 |
  | Specific | 18/19 | 0.642 |
  | Misleading info | 17/19 | 0.683 |
  | Tone | 15/19 | 0.467 |

  - Interpretation (Landis & Koch): Level-of-detail is "fair" and Tone "moderate"; "Overall, there is substantial agreement, since we disagreed on only 13 of 152 values. We discussed differences, reached a consensus, and one author rated the remaining 29 hints" (§3.3.2). — [R24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10055)
- **[B25]** Reliability: "two measures for inter-rater reliability in comparative judgement may be used: Reliability (a per-task measure) and Infit (a per-judge or per-item measure). Our Reliability scores for the four Snapshots were 0.73, 0.76, 0.76 and 0.60. A reliability of 0.7 is generally considered sufficient" (§4.5, p.12). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- **[B25]** inconsistent judges were not excluded: an inconsistent judge is "just one whose opinions differ from the other judges". A linear regression of Infit on judging speed was non-significant (p = 0.693), and experience had no effect on Infit (p = 0.60) (§4.5, §5). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- **[B25]** researcher tagging of hint categories: "Two researchers initially tagged some hints which were unused for the study, in order to calibrate. Then they both categorised all 100 hints… In their initial independent tagging the researchers reached an agreement of 65%. The disputes were resolved in a meeting between the two researchers" (§4.7). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)

### Inferences
- A defensible minimum for a small repair study, following these precedents:
  - two raters independently rate a subset of about 20–100 items (or all items if the budget allows);
  - report Cohen's kappa per attribute, interpreted with Landis & Koch;
  - resolve disagreements by discussion;
  - then either average the two raters ([P24]) or have one rater finish the rest ([PyFiXV], [R24]).
- Expect lower kappa on subjective dimensions: [R24]'s Level-of-detail and Tone were 0.39 and 0.47. "Preserves student intent" may behave the same way, so a calibration round on held-out items, as [B25] did, is worth budgeting.

### Gaps
- [P24] does not say which attribute(s) the kappa of 0.65 was computed on, or over how many items.
- No paper reports Krippendorff's alpha or Fleiss' kappa. With only 2 raters, Cohen's kappa is the default throughout.

## Q6. Effort/time per item and ethics/IRB statements

### Takeaway
The Phung/Singla papers report neither rating time nor any ethics/IRB statement. [R24] reports a 1-hour session (45 minutes on task) and an Utrecht University "Ethics and Privacy Quick Scan" (low risk, no full review). [B25] reports detailed timing (about 75 s for the first judgement, falling to about 20 s), a KCL ethics approval number, and OSF pre-registration with listed deviations.

### Cited Findings
- **[P23]**: no time per item and no ethics or IRB statement found in the text. The only effort-related remark is the GPT-4 rate cap (footnote 5). Funding: ERC TOPS 101039090. — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- **[P24]**: no time per item and no ethics statement. The authors note that the data come from an online master's course; illustrative programs are "slightly obfuscate[d]… to avoid" identification (footnote 1). — [P24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03780)
- **[PyFiXV]**: no rating time reported. The TigerJython data are "a private and anonymized version… with string literals… replaced with sequences of 'x'" (§4.2). No IRB statement. — [PyFiXV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04662)
- **[R24]** ethics: "we evaluated our method with the Ethics and Privacy Quick Scan of Utrecht University. They classified this research as low-risk, with no fuller ethics review or privacy assessment required" (§3.3.1). — [R24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10055)
- **[R24]** time: "The duration of the experiment was roughly one hour… The students worked for 45 minutes on the exercise." Each student received a random number for saving data (§3.3.1). — [R24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10055)
- **[B25]** ethics: "The study was approved according to the ethics procedures of King's College London, approval number MRA-23/24-41449." Pre-registered on OSF, with deviations listed: one Snapshot per educator, human hints added, and a new feedback-literacy coding (§3.6). Open data on OSF (§4.1). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- **[B25]** time per judgement: "≈75 seconds" for the first judgement and "≈50 seconds" for the second, then "a gradual speeding up (from around 40 seconds to around 20 seconds)" (§4.5). A regression of time on combined word count gave 100 words: 28 s, 200: 31 s, 300: 36 s, 400: 41 s, 500: 46 s. The authors conclude "participants were not fully reading most of the hints", and argue this is still valid (§4.5). — [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)
- **[B25]** workload and recruitment issues:
  - 31 comparisons per judge.
  - Task description of "only 2-3 relatively sparse pages".
  - About 50% of sign-ups completed, which the authors attribute to recruiting in August–September during term start (§3.4, §7).
  - Survey: 18/35 found the task easy, 7 medium, 8 hard; only one mentioned boredom (§4.6).
  - Source: [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)

### Inferences
- From [B25]'s figures, 31 judgements at about 20–40 s each is roughly 15–20 minutes of judging per educator, plus reading the Snapshot. This is my rough estimate and is not stated in the paper. It suggests a pairwise design keeps each expert's load under 30 minutes.
- If the planned study uses other students' code (for example, CodeNet trajectories) and external expert raters, [B25] and [R24] show that ethics clearance (even a "quick scan") and pre-registration are now expected in CS-education venues, while the Phung/Singla papers in EDM/LAK/ICER-poster venues omitted them.

### Gaps
- None of the Phung/Singla papers report expert time or effort per item, total annotation hours, or ethics review.

## Q7. How results were reported (tables, statistical tests)

### Takeaway
The Phung/Singla papers report percentages of binary attributes per method as **mean (stderr) across evaluators**, in tables and bar charts per problem. Significance is reported only via **χ² tests on contingency tables** ([P24], [PyFiXV]), and not at all in [P23]. [R24] reports frequencies and Likert distributions with no tests. [B25] uses within-snapshot RankScore (1–25), violin plots per generator and prompt, and a **random-forest** importance analysis (%IncMSE) of hint attributes, plus χ² and linear regressions for validity checks.

### Cited Findings
- **[P23]**: per-scenario tables (Fig. 5a, 7a, …) with "mean (stderr)" across the 2 evaluators, plus per-problem bar charts (Fig. 5b, 7b). Narrative comparisons only, for example "GPT-4 (36.6) does a lot more edits when fixing buggy programs in contrast to that made by Tutor (19.0)" (§3). No statistical tests. — [P23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17156)
- **[P24]** Fig. 6, Precision (Coverage):

  | Technique | BasicAlgo | DataRegex | DataAnalysis |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | GPT4Hints-Base | 66.0 (100) | 85.4 (100) | 78.3 (100) |
  | TutorHints | 92.0 | 91.7 | 91.7 |
  | GPT4Hints-GPT3.5Val | 94.7 (76.0) | 97.6 (87.5) | 95.5 (73.3) |

  - Fig. 8 gives fine-grained per-attribute percentages.
  - Footnote 10: "significantly different w.r.t. χ² tests (p ≤ 0.0001); here, we use contingency tables with two rows (techniques) and four columns (data points are mapped to four possible precision/coverage outcomes)." — [P24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03780)
- **[PyFiXV]**:
  - Precision/coverage table (Fig. 6a) and precision-coverage trade-off curves (Fig. 6b–c).
  - Footnote 9 reports χ² tests with the same 2×4 contingency design (p ≤ 0.0001).
  - Footnote 10 reports F1 of the validation mechanisms against expert ratings: 0.56/0.39/0.70/0.86 (TigerJython) and 0.71/0.47/0.77/0.84 (Codeforces).
  - Source: [PyFiXV](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04662)
- **[R24]**:
  - Student Likert distributions (Fig. 4, n=48): students "often marked the hints as clear and suitable for their work… Only about half of the time, they tagged the hints as helpful" (§5.2).
  - Expert frequency charts for type, level of detail and tone (Fig. 5), additional information (Fig. 6) and binary criteria (Fig. 7).
  - Qualitative examples of misleading hints (§5.3). No inferential statistics.
  - Threats: "only three students… we cannot make strong statements about the effect of the hints" (§6.2.2).
  - Source: [R24](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10055)
- **[B25]**:
  - Scoring: the scaled score (0–100) was rejected because outlier hints in Snapshots 2–3 warp the scale. Ranks were used instead: "RankScore" 25 = best and 1 = worst within a snapshot, with no ties because choices are forced (§4.8–4.9).
  - Generator comparison by violin plots (Fig. 8–10): GPT-4 has the highest mean rank, followed by humans; Mixtral-8x7B is worst; prompt 3 is best and prompt 4 worst. The authors caution that each Generator has only 4 data points (§4.10).
  - Random forest with 500 subsets, importance = %IncMSE (Table 5) (§4.11). Headline findings:
    - 80–160 words is best;
    - reading grade ≤ 9 is best;
    - "Opening up a new perspective" hints rank about 2 places lower;
    - "Guiding" hints rank about 2 places higher;
    - no sentiment effect (abstract, §4.11).
  - Source: [B25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18151)

### Inferences
- To be directly comparable with [P23]/[P24], a histra expert study could report a per-system table of binary-attribute percentages as mean (stderr) across 2 raters, with Correct and EditTokens alongside, and a χ² test (or McNemar for paired binary outcomes on the same programs, which these papers did not use) for system differences.
- If a pairwise or ranking design is chosen, [B25]'s RankScore and "beware of scaled-score warping by outliers" are directly reusable reporting conventions.

### Gaps
- No paper in this set reports confidence intervals, paired tests, or mixed-effects models over raters × items. Statistical rigor in the Phung/Singla line is limited to χ² on contingency tables.
- [R24] Figure 4's exact Likert percentages could not be reliably extracted from the PDF text layer. Only the narrative summary above is safe to quote.
